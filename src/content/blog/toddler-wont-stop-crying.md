## The short answer

When a toddler won't stop crying, first check the basics (hunger, tiredness, pain, a wet nappy), then stay close, keep your own voice low and slow, and name what you think they feel. You don't have to stop the tears quickly. Your calm presence is what helps a small body settle, and most crying spells pass once the feeling has had somewhere to go.

## Why toddlers cry so much

If it feels like your toddler cries at everything, you are not imagining it, and you are not doing anything wrong. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) explains that two-year-olds often swing from cheerful to tearful quickly, sometimes for no clear reason, and that anger and frustration "tend to erupt suddenly in the form of crying, hitting or screaming." They describe this as a normal part of growing up.

There are a few reasons this age is so teary:

- **The feelings are bigger than the words.** The NHS notes that toddlers want to express themselves but find it difficult, and frustration is a common trigger.
- **The brakes aren't built yet.** ZERO TO THREE explains that a child may be able to repeat a rule but usually can't reliably act on it until around three and a half to four years old.
- **Bodies run out of fuel.** Tiredness and hunger are well-known triggers, according to both the NHS and the AAP.

So the crying is not a test you are failing. It is a child doing the only thing they can with a feeling that is too big for them right now.

## Step 1: Check the body first

Before anything else, run through a quick list. It takes ten seconds and often solves the mystery.

- Hungry or thirsty?
- Overtired, or past nap time?
- Too hot, too cold, or uncomfortable clothing?
- Wet or dirty nappy?
- Any sign of pain, a bump, fever or feeling unwell?
- Too much noise, too many people, too long in the car?

If you find something, sort it out first. A snack or a quiet room can do more than any clever phrase.

## Step 2: Get close and get calm

Once you know your child is safe and their basic needs are met, your job is to be a steady presence. ZERO TO THREE points out that adults cannot force a child to calm down, but we can respond in ways that help them settle more quickly. Staying calm and present is the first of those.

Try this:

- Get down to their eye level, or sit nearby on the floor.
- Lower your voice and slow your breathing. Your child reads your body more than your words.
- Offer touch, but let them choose. Some children want a cuddle straight away. Others need you a little further away until the wave passes.
- Use very few words. A crying toddler can't process long explanations.

## Step 3: Name the feeling

The CDC's emotion-coaching guidance suggests noticing, connecting, listening and then naming the emotion "with simple, descriptive words." You don't need to be sure you're right. Saying "It seems like you are upset" still shows you are paying attention.

Phrases you can try:

- "You're so sad. I'm right here."
- "You wanted the red cup. That's really disappointing."
- "Your body is having a big cry. I'll stay with you."
- "You're tired and everything feels hard."

If your toddler pushes back or corrects you, that's fine. Just try again. For more phrases like these, see [what to say instead of "stop crying"](/blog/what-to-say-instead-of-stop-crying/).

## Step 4: Let the feeling finish

This is the hard part. Once you have checked needs, stayed close and named the feeling, sometimes the most useful thing is to wait. The AAP suggests staying calm and letting a tantrum run its course when it is safe to do so.

Waiting is not ignoring. You are still there, still warm, still available. You are simply not rushing to make the feeling disappear. The CDC notes that jumping into problem-solving too soon can unintentionally dismiss a child's emotions.

Think of Blu in the hospital corridor, crying as Dad holds him close. Dad doesn't need to explain anything or fix the moment. He just holds on until the worry has room to settle. That is the whole job.

## Step 5: Reconnect and move on

When the crying slows, you might notice a long sigh, a body that softens, or eyes that look around again. This is your moment to reconnect.

- Offer a hug, a drink of water or a quiet song.
- Name what happened in one short sentence: "That was a big sad. You were so upset the tower fell."
- Then gently move to something new: "Shall we build it again, or read a book?"

Keep this light. Your toddler doesn't need a lesson. They need to know the feeling came and went, and you were there the whole time.

## What usually makes crying last longer

Most of us have done all of these on a hard day. They're worth knowing, not worth feeling guilty about.

- **Shouting back.** The NHS is clear that losing your temper or shouting will not end a tantrum.
- **Long explanations** while your child is still upset.
- **Telling them to stop feeling it.** The CDC advises against insisting a child stop feeling a certain way.
- **Giving in to end the noise.** The NHS notes that if you give in, your child may learn that crying gets results.

## When you are running on empty

Constant crying is draining. If you can feel your own temper rising, it is okay to step back. Make sure your child is somewhere safe, then take a few breaths in another room or hand over to another adult if one is around. The AAP suggests taking breaks for exactly this reason. Looking after yourself is part of looking after them.

## When to talk to your paediatrician

This article is general information, not medical advice. Please speak to your paediatrician or health visitor if:

- Crying seems linked to pain, illness, or comes with a fever or other symptoms.
- Your child seems very withdrawn, constantly sad, or demanding and unsatisfied most of the time. The AAP notes these can sometimes point to a deeper problem.
- Crying spells are much longer or more frequent than usual, or cause harm. UNICEF India advises seeking professional advice if tantrums are frequent, disrupt daily routines or cause harm.
- You simply feel something isn't right. You know your child best.

Most toddler crying is a healthy, if loud, sign of a child learning to feel. With time, words and a lot of patient company from you, it does get easier.
