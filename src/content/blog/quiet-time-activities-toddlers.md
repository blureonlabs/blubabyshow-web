## The short answer

For ten quiet minutes, give your toddler one simple, absorbing task close to where you are: a posting box, a sorting tray, stickers, a basket of books or a colouring page. Set it up before you need it, keep it within sight, and do not expect long stretches from very young toddlers. Short is normal.

## Be realistic about attention spans

Ten minutes can feel like nothing to an adult and like forever to a toddler. The CDC's milestone checklist lists "pays attention for 5 to 10 minutes during activities" as something children typically do by age 5. So if your 2-year-old drifts off after three minutes, that is not a failure. It is just two.

The trick is not finding one activity that lasts ten minutes. It is having two or three short ones ready, and starting them before you are desperate.

## Before you start

- **Pick the spot.** Quiet time works best within sight of where you will be: the kitchen floor while you cook, a mat beside your desk, the bedroom floor while you feed the baby.
- **Safety-check it.** Remove anything that fits fully in your child's mouth. The American Academy of Pediatrics lists coins, button batteries, pen caps, marbles and small toy parts as common hazards.
- **Keep a quiet-time basket.** A box of activities that only comes out at these moments stays interesting for longer.

## 12 quiet-time activities

Ages are rough guides. Follow your child's interest and how they handle objects, especially whether they still mouth things.

### 12 to 24 months

1. **Posting box.** Cut a slot in the lid of a shoebox or an empty formula tin and give your toddler large jar lids, old cards or wooden spoons to post through. Big items only.
2. **Tissue box pull.** Stuff an empty tissue box with scarves or cloth napkins to pull out, one by one. Then stuff them back in.
3. **Cupboard of safe things.** Choose one low kitchen cupboard and fill it with steel bowls, plastic containers and lids. Toddlers love emptying it. Make sure every other low cupboard is locked or cleared of cleaning products and medicines.
4. **Board books on the floor.** Spread four or five sturdy books face-up on a mat. Many toddlers will turn pages on their own for a few minutes, especially with lift-the-flap books.

### 2 to 3 years

5. **Sorting tray.** Put mixed items in a muffin tin or a few bowls: clothes pegs, jar lids, socks, spoons. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child suggests sorting games by size, shape or colour for 18- to 36-month-olds, because the child has to hold a rule in mind and follow it.
6. **Sticker sheet.** Large stickers on a sheet of paper keep small fingers busy. Peeling is hard work at this age, so lift the corners first.
7. **Colouring page.** One page and two or three chunky crayons is plenty. A page with a familiar character often holds attention longer.
8. **Toy wash.** A damp cloth and a few plastic toys or animals to "clean". This one uses a cloth, not a bowl of water, so you are not leaving water out.

### 3 to 4 years

9. **Pretend picnic.** A cloth, some plastic plates and a few soft toys to feed. The Harvard guide includes simple imaginary play among its activities for building self-control at this age, and suggests letting the child direct the game.
10. **Picture hunt.** Give your child a busy picture book and something to find: "How many dogs can you see?"
11. **Simple puzzle.** A four- to eight-piece puzzle is a good challenge.
12. **Story drawing.** Ask them to draw what happened at the park this morning. The Harvard guide notes that telling stories about shared events helps children reflect on them.

## Making it work

- **Stay connected.** A quick comment every minute or two keeps them going: "You found a red one!" The Harvard guide suggests narrating children's play as a way to help them link words to their actions.
- **Start small.** Begin with three minutes and build up. Praise the quiet, not just the result.
- **Rotate.** Swap the basket contents every week so the activities feel new.
- **Finish well.** Come back before they fall apart, so quiet time ends on a good note.

## When there is a new baby

Quiet time is often most needed when a newborn arrives and a parent is feeding or settling the baby. In one of our episodes, Blu gently kisses his sleeping baby sister, and those calm moments do not happen by chance. Giving a toddler a special "baby time basket" that only comes out during feeds can make them feel included rather than pushed aside. Sit them close enough to see the baby and chat with them while you feed.

## Where screens fit

Sometimes a show is the right tool, and that is fine. Screens can be part of a balanced day. The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests using screens at regular times and trying not to hand over a device every time a child is upset or bored, so these quiet activities give you options for the in-between moments. Ten minutes of calm is a good result, even if it took three different activities and you were interrupted twice. You are teaching your child to settle and play alongside you, which is a skill that grows slowly.
