## The short answer

Teach your toddler to name their feelings by naming them out loud, many times a day, in short and simple words: "You're happy!", "That made you sad." Name your own feelings and those of characters in books too. Toddlers learn feeling words the same way they learn "cup" and "dog": by hearing them, over and over, linked to real moments.

## Why feeling words matter so early

A toddler who can say "mad" has another option besides throwing the cup. That's the practical reason. But there's more going on underneath.

Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes "serve and return", the back-and-forth between a child and a caring adult, as one of the ways young brains are built. They point out that naming what a child sees, does or feels helps build early language connections, even before the child can talk.

There's also some interesting adult research. A 2007 brain-imaging study at UCLA found that when adults labelled emotions in words, activity in the amygdala (involved in emotional reactions) was reduced. That was in grown-ups, so it isn't proof of what happens in a toddler's brain. Still, it lines up with what the CDC recommends for young children: naming a feeling is part of helping a child manage it.

## What to expect by age two

Every child develops at their own pace. The CDC's milestone checklist for two-year-olds includes a couple of signs linked to feelings and words:

- Notices when others are hurt or upset, for example pausing or looking sad when someone is crying.
- Looks at your face to see how to react in a new situation.
- Says at least two words together, like "More milk."

So at two, most toddlers are reading feelings in faces before they can name many. Feeling words usually follow, slowly, with lots of repetition. That's normal.

## Start with four feelings

You don't need a chart of thirty emotions. Start with a small, clear set and use them often:

- **Happy**
- **Sad**
- **Angry** (or "mad", or "cross")
- **Scared**

Once these feel familiar, you can add more: tired, frustrated, excited, proud, silly, worried, disappointed, lonely.

## Everyday ways to name feelings

### Narrate feelings as they happen

This is the most powerful tool, and it's free. When you see a feeling, say it.

- "You're laughing! You're so happy."
- "The tower fell. You look frustrated."
- "You jumped at that loud bang. That was scary."
- "You're hugging Puppy. You love him."

The CDC suggests guessing gently when you're not sure: "It looks like something is bothering you." If your child corrects you, try again with their word.

### Name your own feelings

Children learn emotion words from how we talk about ourselves too.

- "I'm feeling a bit cross. I'm going to take a big breath."
- "I'm so excited Grandma is coming!"
- "I feel sad that it's raining. I wanted to go outside."

Keep it light and brief. The aim is to show that grown-ups have feelings too, and that feelings have names.

### Use mirror play

Pull silly faces together in a mirror. "Show me a happy face! Now a sad face. Now a surprised face!" Toddlers love seeing themselves change. In one Blu episode, Blu catches sight of himself in the bathroom mirror with a toothpaste-foam mohawk and bursts out laughing. A moment like that is a perfect chance to say, "Look at that face. That's a happy, silly face."

### Read and point

Picture books are full of feelings. Pause on a page and ask:

- "How does the bear feel? Is he sad?"
- "Look at her face. What do you think happened?"
- "Have you ever felt like that?"

The CDC milestone list notes that two-year-olds can point to things in a book when asked. Feeling faces are a natural next step.

### Draw and colour feelings

Simple drawings of faces (a smiley mouth, a down-turned mouth, big round eyes for scared) give toddlers something to point at when the words won't come. Some families keep a small set of feeling faces on the fridge. If you'd like ready-made ones, we have feelings colouring pages in the [shop](/shop/).

### Sing it

Change the words of a song your child already knows: "If you're happy and you know it" works for sad, angry and scared too, with a matching action for each (stamp for angry, hug yourself for scared).

## Naming feelings in hard moments

When your toddler is mid-meltdown, keep it very short. The CDC suggests that if a child is too distressed, you first make sure everyone is safe and wait until you're both calmer.

During:

- "You're so angry."
- "Big sad. I'm here."

After, once they've settled:

- "You were really cross when we left the park. Your face went red and you shouted. That's angry."

Talking it through afterwards, when your child can actually listen, often teaches more than anything said during the storm. For more on staying steady in those moments, see our guide to [toddler tantrums and how to respond](/blog/toddler-tantrums-why-and-how/).

## Accept every feeling, guide the behaviour

Naming a feeling doesn't mean allowing everything that comes with it. The CDC's model is simple: accept the emotion, then set limits on behaviour. "It's ok to feel angry, but it's not ok to throw our blocks."

This teaches a powerful idea: feelings are always allowed. Some actions aren't.

## When to check in with your doctor

This is general information, not medical advice. The CDC encourages parents to act early. Talk to your paediatrician if your child isn't meeting milestones for their age, such as using two words together by two, if they've lost skills they used to have, or if you have any other worries about how they communicate or connect with others. You know your child best, and asking early is always okay.
