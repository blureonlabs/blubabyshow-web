## The short answer

The best way to count with a toddler is to count real things you are already doing: stairs, spoons, buttons, toys going into a box. Toddlers often say "one, two, three" long before they understand what the words mean, and that is normal. Learning numbers takes years, and everyday talk about more, less, big and small builds the groundwork.

## How toddlers learn numbers

Saying number words and understanding them are two different skills. Many toddlers can chant "one, two, three, four" like a song. Ask them for "three spoons", though, and they may hand you a whole fistful.

Developmental psychologist Barbara Sarnecka, reviewing research on how children learn number words, describes a step-by-step process. Children first learn exactly what "one" means, then, months later, "two", then "three". In the most studied group of children, they typically reach the "one" stage somewhere between about 2 and 4 years old, and fully understand that the last number you count tells you "how many" about a year later. The age varies from child to child.

So your toddler is not "bad at counting". They are doing exactly what young children do: learning the sounds first and the meaning slowly.

## What the milestones say

The CDC lists "counts to 10" and "names some numbers between 1 and 5 when you point to them" among the things most children do by 5 years. That gives a sense of how gradual this is. There is no need to rush.

It also helps to know what counting really involves. To count five blocks, a child has to say the number words in the right order, match exactly one word to each block, keep track of which blocks they have already counted, and understand that the last word they said is the total. That is a lot for a small brain to juggle at once. Each everyday counting moment practises one or two of those pieces, and over time they come together.

## Why everyday maths talk matters

ZERO TO THREE, an early childhood organisation, notes that using simple maths language with babies and toddlers helps build both early maths knowledge and vocabulary. It points to early research finding that toddlers who heard more maths language from their parents had better vocabulary and some stronger early maths skills in preschool.

Maths talk is not only numbers. It includes:

- **Quantity:** more, less, a lot, a little, all gone
- **Size:** big, small, tall, short
- **Position:** in, on, under, next to
- **Patterns:** first, then, next

## 12 everyday ways to count

Ages are rough guides. Follow your child's interest.

### 12 to 24 months

1. **Count body parts.** "One nose. Two eyes. Ten toes." ZERO TO THREE suggests counting body parts, on your child and on their toys.
2. **Count stairs.** Say a number for each step as you climb together.
3. **"More" and "all gone".** At meals, talk about more rice, less water, and food that is all gone. These are early ideas of quantity.
4. **Action rhymes with numbers.** Number songs and finger rhymes in any language repeat the counting words in a fun, predictable way.

### 2 to 3 years

5. **Count into a box.** At tidy-up time, count toys as they go into the box: "One car, two cars, three cars."
6. **Lay the table.** Ask your toddler to give everyone one spoon. Matching one object to one person is an important early counting skill.
7. **Which pile has more?** Make two piles of grapes cut small, or blocks, and ask which has more. ZERO TO THREE suggests this kind of question.
8. **Sort, then count.** Harvard's Center on the Developing Child suggests sorting games by size, shape or colour for 18- to 36-month-olds. Sort socks into two piles, then count each pile.

### 3 to 4 years

9. **Count and stop.** Ask for exactly three blocks, then check together by touching each one as you count.
10. **Add one, take one away.** ZERO TO THREE suggests narrating changes in quantity: "You had three banana slices. You ate one. Now there are two."
11. **Counting walks.** Count red cars, dogs or birds on a short walk.
12. **Kitchen measuring.** Let your child count scoops of flour into a bowl while you bake. In one of our episodes, Blu and the Puppy leave a trail of floury paw prints across the kitchen floor. Counting paw prints, or footprints in the sand, is a fun way to practise with something they made themselves.

## Small tips that help

- **Touch as you count.** Point to or move each object as you say the number. This helps children connect one word to one thing.
- **Repeat the last number.** After counting, say "Three! There are three cars." This highlights that the last number tells you how many.
- **Do not correct every slip.** Skipping "four" or counting the same block twice is normal. Model the right way and move on.
- **Use your home language.** Count in whatever language feels natural to your family, or in more than one.
- **Keep it short.** A few counting moments woven into the day are better than one long lesson.

## When to check in

Counting develops slowly and at very different speeds. On its own, not counting yet is rarely a concern for a toddler. If you have broader worries about your child's language, understanding or development, the CDC encourages talking with your child's doctor and asking about developmental screening rather than waiting.
