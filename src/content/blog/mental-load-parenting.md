## The short answer

The mental load is the invisible work of running a family: noticing the nappies are low, remembering the vaccination date, knowing the shoes no longer fit. Research shows it is real work, often unevenly shared, and linked to lower wellbeing when one parent feels solely responsible. The fix is not to try harder; it is to make the load visible and hand over whole tasks, thinking included.

## The work no one sees

Before you had children, someone may have warned you about the nappies and the sleepless nights. Fewer people mention the list. The never-ending, always-running list in your head:

- We are nearly out of wipes.
- The baby has outgrown the 0–3 month vests.
- The vaccination appointment is next Thursday. Who is taking her?
- Grandma's birthday is on Sunday. Card? Call?
- The toddler has been coughing at night for three days. Is that normal?
- Nursery wants a spare set of clothes by Friday.

None of this shows up on a chore chart. But it takes time, energy and attention, and it often runs at 2am.

## What researchers call it

In a 2019 study in the *American Sociological Review*, sociologist Allison Daminger interviewed 70 people in 35 couples about how they run their homes. She called this invisible thinking work "cognitive labour" and found it has four parts:

1. **Anticipating needs**: noticing that something will be needed
2. **Identifying options**: researching what could meet that need
3. **Deciding**: choosing among the options
4. **Monitoring**: checking that it happened and worked

The study found that women in these couples did more cognitive labour overall, especially the anticipating and monitoring. Decision-making was shared roughly equally. As JSTOR Daily's summary puts it, much of the noticing and follow-up goes unseen when couples describe how they make decisions together.

That pattern explains a common frustration. One parent may happily help choose a nursery, but the other spent weeks noticing the need, shortlisting, visiting and following up.

## Why it matters for wellbeing

A 2019 study in *Sex Roles* by Lucia Ciciolla and Suniya Luthar surveyed 393 mothers in the United States, mostly married or partnered and upper-middle-class. Many reported being mainly responsible for running the household. Feeling primarily responsible for children's wellbeing in particular was linked to lower satisfaction with life and with their partner, and to greater feelings of emptiness.

Two honest caveats: the study shows an association, not cause and effect, and its sample was not representative of all families. Still, it matches what many parents describe: the weight is not only in the doing but in being the one who always has to remember.

The early years also come with real strain on mental health. Postpartum Support International notes that 1 in 5 mums and dads report depressive or anxiety symptoms in the first year after birth. A heavy, unshared mental load does not help. If you are struggling, please read [our note for the parent crying in the bathroom](/blog/crying-in-the-bathroom-postpartum/), and talk to your doctor.

## This is not about blame

The mental load is not a "mum problem" or a "bad partner problem". It grows quietly. Often one parent is home more in the first months, so they start noticing first. Then the noticing becomes their job without anyone deciding it. Plenty of dads and partners carry it too, especially in families where roles are flipped or shared differently.

The goal is not a perfect 50/50 split of every single task. It is a split that both of you can see, agree on and adjust.

## How to share the mental load

### 1. Make the invisible list visible

Sit down together for twenty minutes and write everything down: meals, laundry, appointments, birthdays, nursery forms, baby clothes sizes, the bin day, the pet's vet visits. Seeing it on paper often surprises both parents.

### 2. Hand over whole tasks, not errands

There is a big difference between "Can you buy nappies?" and "You own nappies." Owning a task means doing all four parts: noticing when stock is low, choosing what to buy, buying it and checking you have enough. Try dividing whole areas:

- One parent owns medical: appointments, vaccinations, the medicine box.
- The other owns food: the shopping list, meal plan and lunchboxes.
- Swap or rebalance every few months.

### 3. Let the other parent do it their way

If you hand over bath time, let bath time look different. A slightly different nappy brand or a different bedtime song is not a failure. Stepping back is part of sharing the load.

### 4. Use one shared system

A shared calendar or a shared note on both phones means no one is the only keeper of the information. "It's in the calendar" is a gift to the other parent.

### 5. Hold a weekly check-in

Ten or fifteen minutes, once a week. What is coming up? What fell through the cracks? Who is taking what? Keep it practical and kind.

### 6. Lower the bar on purpose

The NHS suggests that tired new parents put off non-essential household chores and accept help from friends and family. Some items on the list can simply be crossed off for now.

## A small scene from Blu

In one of our episodes, Blu and his Dad end up dusted head to toe in flour, with Puppy's paw prints all over the kitchen, while Mum appears in the doorway. It is played for laughs, and it is funny. But the scene also hints at the question every household knows: who notices the mess, and who sorts it out? In Blu's world, Dad is the hands-on yes-parent who joins the chaos. In real homes, the aim is that he also helps clean it up, and maybe notices the flour is running low.

## Words that help

If you are the one carrying more:

- "I don't need help. I need you to own this part, start to finish."
- "Can we look at the list together on Sunday?"

If you are the one who wants to carry more:

- "What's on your mind this week that I could take off it?"
- "I'll handle all the doctor stuff from now on. Tell me where things are."

The mental load never fully disappears in a family with small children. But shared, it is lighter, and it leaves more room for the good parts.
