## The short answer

If you are crying in the bathroom with the tap running so no one hears, you are not failing, and you are not alone. Some tears after a baby are common. But if the sadness, worry or numbness lasts more than about two weeks, or makes it hard to cope, it may be postpartum depression or anxiety, which are common and treatable. Please talk to your doctor, midwife or health visitor. If you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, contact your local emergency services now.

## A note first

This is for the parent sitting on the edge of the bath, or on the floor with your back against the door. Maybe the baby is finally asleep. Maybe someone else is holding them and you said you needed the loo, because the bathroom is the only room with a lock.

Whatever brought you here, you deserve care too. Not after the washing. Not when the baby sleeps through. Now.

You might be a new mum. You might be a dad, a partner, a second parent who feels they should be the strong one. This note is for you too.

## Some tears are normal

The first days after birth bring enormous change: hormones, healing, broken sleep and a brand-new person to keep alive. The NHS says that feeling low, anxious or irritable in the first few days after the birth is common, and is sometimes called the baby blues. It usually goes away within two weeks.

So if you cried on day four because the toast was cold, that may simply be the blues.

## When it may be more than the blues

Postpartum depression (also called postnatal depression) is common. The World Health Organization estimates that worldwide, about 13% of women who have just given birth experience a mental disorder, mainly depression, and in developing countries the figure is higher, close to 20%. The CDC reports that about 1 in 8 women with a recent live birth in the United States reported symptoms of postpartum depression. And it is not only mothers: Postpartum Support International notes that 1 in 5 mums and dads report depressive or anxiety symptoms in the first year after birth.

The NHS lists these signs of postnatal depression:

- A persistent low mood, or feeling sad
- Losing interest in things, or no longer enjoying them
- Feeling hopeless, or unable to cope
- Feeling guilty, or like a bad parent
- Feeling anxious, restless or irritable
- Trouble sleeping, even when the baby sleeps
- Finding it hard to concentrate
- Difficulty bonding with your baby
- Thoughts of suicide, or of harming yourself or your baby

The CDC adds crying more often than usual, feeling angry and feeling distant from your baby. The NHS notes that symptoms can start during pregnancy, soon after birth, or up to a year later.

### Postpartum anxiety

Anxiety after a baby is just as real, and it can look different. Postpartum Support International describes perinatal anxiety as including:

- Constant worry
- A feeling that something bad is going to happen
- Racing thoughts
- Changes in sleep and appetite
- Restlessness and irritability
- Physical symptoms such as dizziness, hot flushes or nausea

If you recognise yourself in these lists, even partly, it is worth speaking to someone. The NHS says it is important to get help even if you only have some of the signs.

## What to do next

**Talk to your doctor.** Your GP, doctor, midwife, obstetrician or health visitor can help. You can say it plainly: "I haven't felt like myself since the baby came. I'm struggling and I'd like help." You do not need to prove anything. Postpartum Support International says you do not need a diagnosis to reach out.

**Know that it is treatable.** The WHO is clear that maternal mental disorders are treatable, and that effective support can be delivered even by trained non-specialist health workers. Postpartum Support International lists social support, counselling and medication evaluation as options. The NHS notes that postnatal depression usually gets better in three to six months, but sometimes lasts longer, which is another reason to get help early.

**Find local support.** Postpartum Support International has a [Find Local Support](https://www.postpartum.net/get-help/locations/) page with regional help for the Americas, Europe, Asia and the Pacific, the Middle East, North Africa and Sub-Saharan Africa.

**Tell one person.** A partner, a sister, a friend. Hand them this page if words are hard.

## If you are thinking of harming yourself or your baby

Please do not wait. Contact your local emergency number, go to your nearest emergency department, or call your doctor straight away. Postpartum Support International advises that people in crisis should call their physician or their local emergency number. These thoughts are a symptom of an illness that can be treated. They do not make you a bad parent, and you deserve immediate help.

## Small things that can help today

These are not a replacement for treatment. They are ways to get through the next hour.

- **Drink a glass of water and eat something.** Low blood sugar makes everything heavier.
- **Hand the baby to someone safe and sleep.** Even one protected stretch of sleep can help.
- **Step outside.** Five minutes of fresh air, with or without the pram.
- **Lower the bar.** The washing can wait. Takeaway is dinner. That is fine.
- **Put the baby somewhere safe and take a breath.** If you feel overwhelmed, it is always okay to lay your baby down in their cot and step away for a few minutes.

## For partners and family

If you are reading this because someone you love keeps disappearing into the bathroom, gently ask: "How are you really doing?" Then listen. Offer to come to the doctor with them. Take a night feed, the morning shift, or the older child to the park. Practical help is love you can see.
