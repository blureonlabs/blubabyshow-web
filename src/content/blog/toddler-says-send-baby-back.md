## The short answer

When your toddler says "send the baby back", they are usually telling you, in the only words they have, that sharing you is hard. Stay calm, name the feeling ("You wish things were like before"), reassure them that they are loved and staying, and offer a moment of closeness. Save limits for unsafe actions, not for feelings.

## Why toddlers say it

It can sting to hear. You have imagined your children as friends, and here is your firstborn asking for a return to sender. But this is one of the most common things young children say after a sibling arrives. ZERO TO THREE even titled its guide on the subject after a version of it: "When is he going back in your belly?" The same guide describes older children announcing that they wish the baby would just go away.

Behind the words is usually something simpler:

- **"I miss you."** The baby takes up a great deal of time and arms.
- **"I'm worried."** Things changed, and they do not know what it means for them.
- **"I'm testing."** Will you still love them if they say the unsayable?
- **"I'm curious."** Some toddlers genuinely think the baby is a visitor.

ZERO TO THREE notes that older children can love the baby and also feel angry or worried about losing attention. Both feelings fit inside one small child.

## What to say in the moment

The aim is not to argue your child out of the feeling. It is to show them the feeling is safe to have, and that they are still deeply loved. Try one of these, in a calm, warm voice.

**Name the feeling**
- "You wish the baby would go back. It is hard having a baby in the house."
- "You miss when it was just you and me. I miss that too sometimes."

**Reassure their place**
- "The baby is staying. And you are staying in my heart forever. There is room for both of you."
- "Nothing will ever change how much I love you."

**Offer connection**
- "Come here. Let's have a big squeeze."
- "When the baby has finished her milk, it is our turn. What shall we play?"

**Add humour if your child is in a light mood**
- "Send her back? But who would you show your dinosaur dance to?"

Keep it short. Toddlers take in a few warm words far better than a long explanation.

## What not to say

Some responses come out of tiredness and love but tend to make the feeling bigger.

- **"That's a horrible thing to say."** ZERO TO THREE warns that shaming a child for jealousy can increase negative feelings towards the baby.
- **"You love your sister, don't you?"** Pressure to love the baby can have the opposite effect.
- **"Big boys don't say that."** Asking a child to be "big" when they feel small tends to backfire.
- **"Fine, we'll send you away instead."** Even as a joke, this lands on a toddler's deepest worry.

## Feelings are welcome; some actions are not

There is a difference between saying "I don't want the baby" and pushing the baby. Words can be met with empathy. Unsafe actions need a calm, firm limit. ZERO TO THREE recommends guiding a child's hands firmly but not angrily, and moving them to another activity if it continues.

- "You can tell me you're cross with the baby. You cannot hit her. Let's stamp our feet instead."
- "I won't let you push. I'll keep everyone safe."

ZERO TO THREE's other guide puts it well: show empathy while still setting appropriate limits, and when a child sees you are compassionate but clear, they are likely to adapt. And whatever the mood in the room, never leave a toddler alone with a baby.

## Give the feeling somewhere to go

Toddlers process big feelings through play and their bodies more than through talk.

- **Draw it.** "Can you draw how you feel about the baby?" Scribbles count.
- **Play it out.** Let your child put a baby doll "to bed" or even "send it away" in play. Pretend play is a safe place for real feelings.
- **Read about it.** Picture books about new siblings help children see that other children feel this way too.
- **Move it.** Jumping, stomping or a run in the garden can release a lot.

In one of our episodes, Blu holds up his top over a rumbling tummy, lip wobbling, with no words for what is going on inside. Toddlers often feel things in their bodies before they can name them, which is why helping them put words to a feeling matters so much.

## Prevent the build-up

Much of the "send the baby back" talk fades when your child feels less squeezed out. A few habits help:

- **Daily one-on-one time.** KidsHealth suggests a stretch of one parent's undivided attention each day.
- **Say the baby needs you, then come back.** HealthyChildren.org suggests explaining that sometimes the baby needs care first, and reminding your toddler they are loved.
- **Let both parents step in.** When Dad or another parent takes over bath or bedtime, the older child gets a steady, loving person of their own.
- **Accept regression for a while.** If your toddler wants to be carried or cuddled like a baby, give them some of that. It usually passes.

For more on spotting and easing jealousy, see our guide to [sibling jealousy in toddlers](/blog/sibling-jealousy-toddler/).

## When to check in with a professional

Most children who say "send the baby back" grow into siblings who would never hear of it. Talk to your paediatrician, GP or health visitor if your toddler keeps trying to hurt the baby despite calm limits, or if their distress, sleep problems or regression go on for a long time or get worse. You do not have to work it out alone.

One last thing: if part of you also misses the old days, that is allowed too. Loving two children is a stretch, and the stretch takes time.
