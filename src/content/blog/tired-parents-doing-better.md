## The short answer

If you are reading this tired, worried that you are not doing enough, you are almost certainly doing better than you think. Exhaustion in the early years is normal, not a sign of failure. The small, ordinary moments you already share with your child, like answering a babble or naming what they see, are exactly what helps a young brain grow.

## For the parent reading this at 3am

Perhaps you are feeding a baby in the dark, scrolling with one thumb. Perhaps you have just got a toddler back to sleep for the third time. Perhaps it is mid-afternoon and you have not sat down since breakfast.

Here is something worth hearing: the NHS says it is completely normal for parents to feel exhausted in the first weeks or months after having a baby. Lack of sleep can leave you physically and emotionally drained. Feeling like this is not a verdict on your parenting. It is what caring for a small human does to a body.

And the NHS adds something you may need to read twice: things will improve.

## Why "good enough" days still count

Many parents carry a quiet scoreboard. Did we read enough books today? Was there too much screen time? Did I lose my temper at bedtime? Was dinner beige again?

It helps to know what actually matters most to a young child's development. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes "serve and return": a baby or toddler reaches out with a babble, a gesture, a look or a cry, and an adult responds with eye contact, words or a hug. According to the Center, these back-and-forth exchanges help build and strengthen the connections in a child's brain, which support communication and social skills.

Now think about your day. You probably:

- Answered a cry, more than once
- Made a silly face back when your baby made one at you
- Said "Yes, that's a dog!" when your toddler pointed
- Named a feeling: "You're cross because the tower fell"
- Hugged someone who bumped their head

Those were not small things. That was the work. The Center notes that naming what a child sees, does or feels helps build early language connections, even before the child can talk.

None of it needed a perfect house, a perfect plan or a perfect mood.

## What you might be doing better than you think

- **You are still showing up.** Tired, impatient, unwashed hair and all.
- **You are noticing.** You worry because you are paying attention.
- **You are repairing.** If you snapped and then said sorry, you showed your child that people can say sorry and make up.
- **You are reading this.** Looking for ways to do well is itself a sign of caring.

This goes for every parent. If you are a dad doing the night feeds, the bath and the nursery run, your tiredness counts too. Children benefit from more than one loving, responsive adult.

## Small ways to make tired days lighter

The NHS offers simple tips for parents coping with tiredness:

- **Rest when your baby sleeps,** even if it is only a short nap
- **Accept or ask for help** from friends and family
- **Put off non-essential chores**
- **Eat healthy, balanced meals**
- **Get outside** for a walk and some fresh air

A few more ideas that many families find useful:

- **Take turns.** If you have a partner, split nights or mornings so each of you gets one protected stretch of sleep.
- **Say yes to imperfect help.** If a grandparent folds the laundry wrong, the laundry is still folded.
- **Shrink the list.** Ask, "What is the one thing that matters today?" Do that. Let the rest wait.
- **Share the thinking, not just the jobs.** Much of parenting tiredness comes from always remembering. Our post on [the mental load no one warned you about](/blog/mental-load-parenting/) has ideas for sharing it.

## Things you can say to yourself

Some parents find it helps to have a few kind words ready for hard moments:

- "This is a hard stage, not a hard life."
- "My child doesn't need a perfect parent. They need me, responding."
- "I lost my patience, and I said sorry. That is good parenting."
- "Rest is part of the job."

## A small moment from Blu

In one of our episodes, Blu shows off an outfit he picked himself: sunglasses, a rainbow bow tie and rainbow trousers. It is a lot. Behind him, Mum simply claps and cheers. It is a reminder that some of the best parenting moments look like doing very little at all: noticing, delighting, letting them be seen.

You have probably had a moment like that this week, even if you were too tired to notice.

## When tiredness is something more

Sometimes exhaustion is more than lack of sleep. The NHS lists signs of postnatal depression that include a persistent low mood, losing interest in things, feeling hopeless or unable to cope, feeling like a bad parent, anxiety, and trouble sleeping even when the baby sleeps. Postpartum Support International notes that 1 in 5 mums and dads report depressive or anxiety symptoms in the first year after birth, and that these conditions are treatable.

If any of this sounds familiar, please talk to your doctor, midwife or health visitor. The NHS says it is important to get help even if you only have some of the signs. Our note [to the parent crying in the bathroom](/blog/crying-in-the-bathroom-postpartum/) has more on the signs and where to find support. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, contact your local emergency services straight away.

## One last thing

Tomorrow you will probably do much of it again: the feeds, the nappies, the endless snacks, the "one more story". And whether or not anyone says it out loud, your child will be learning, every day, that someone answers when they call.

That someone is you. You're doing better than you think.
