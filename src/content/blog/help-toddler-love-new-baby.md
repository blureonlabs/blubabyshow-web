## The short answer

You cannot make a toddler love a new baby, but you can make love easier to grow. Take the pressure off, give your child small real jobs, teach gentle touch, protect your one-on-one time with them, and notice every small kindness out loud.

## First, a reality check

Parents often hope for an instant bond. For a two-year-old, a newborn is a strange arrival: it cries, it sleeps, it cannot play, and it takes up a great deal of Mum's and Dad's time. ZERO TO THREE points out that early on babies give very little back, so expecting immediate affection is unrealistic.

Mixed feelings are normal. The same child who strokes the baby's foot in the morning may wish out loud, by the afternoon, that the baby would go away. Both are true at once. If that second feeling is the one you are hearing most, our post on [what to say when your toddler says "send the baby back"](/blog/toddler-says-send-baby-back/) has word-for-word replies.

With that in mind, here are five gentle ways to help the bond grow.

## 1. Take the pressure off

It is tempting to say "Give your sister a kiss" or "You love the baby, don't you?" many times a day. ZERO TO THREE found that when an older child senses pressure to love the baby, it can have the opposite effect and make them less likely to feel warmly towards their sibling.

Instead, let affection be optional and interest be welcome.

- Swap "Kiss the baby" for "The baby is looking at you. Do you want to say hello?"
- Swap "You love him, don't you?" for "He is very small. What do you notice about his fingers?"
- If your child walks away, let them. Curiosity often comes back on its own.

## 2. Give them real, small jobs

Toddlers love to be useful. UNICEF suggests involving younger children in baby care, such as fetching nappies, and older children in more hands-on help. Australia's Pregnancy, Birth and Baby service suggests similar tasks, like bringing a nappy or wipes.

Toddler-sized jobs:

- Fetching a clean nappy or a muslin cloth
- Choosing between two baby vests: "This one or this one?"
- Singing to the baby during a nappy change
- Turning on the bath tap with you (and testing the water together)
- Pushing the pram for a few steps on a flat path, with your hand on it

ZERO TO THREE adds an important note: invite help, but accept a "no" calmly. Shaming a child for not wanting to help can increase negative feelings towards the baby.

## 3. Teach gentle touch, without fear

Toddlers want to touch the baby, and they are not always gentle. ZERO TO THREE suggests practising on a doll or soft toy first, showing gentle versus rough. When your child is rough with the real baby, calmly guide their hands, firmly but not angrily, and frame it around the rule rather than casting them as a danger.

What that sounds like:

- "Gentle hands. Like this. One finger on her foot."
- "We touch babies softly. Let's stroke her blanket together."
- "Hands are not for pushing. Come and help me with the blocks instead."

For holding, the Australian service suggests letting your toddler hold the baby with your help, sitting on your lap. Keep it short and supported.

One rule never bends: never leave a toddler alone with a baby, even for a moment. The same service notes that a toddler might share food the baby could choke on, or rock a pram until it tips.

## 4. Protect one-on-one time

Much sibling resentment is really about lost attention. HealthyChildren.org, from the American Academy of Pediatrics, recommends making sure the focus is not all on the new baby and planning one-on-one time with your toddler, which grandparents and relatives can share. UNICEF suggests the same, and asks relatives to do it too.

This works best when it is predictable. A few ideas:

- **The baby-nap date.** When the baby sleeps, put your phone away and play whatever your toddler chooses for ten minutes.
- **A parent-and-me outing.** Dad takes the toddler to the park every Saturday morning, just the two of them.
- **Narrate the wait.** HealthyChildren.org suggests explaining that sometimes the baby needs care first. ZERO TO THREE offers a model: "It is so hard to wait while I feed your sister. I will help you build your tower when she is done eating." Then keep the promise.
- **Visitors say hello to the big one first.** Ask guests to greet your toddler before the baby, and spend time with them.

## 5. Notice the small kindnesses

UNICEF recommends praising loving behaviour towards the baby and telling your child what a great big brother or sister they are. Keep it specific, so your child knows exactly what you saw:

- "You brought her the blanket. That was kind."
- "The baby stopped crying when you sang. She likes your voice."
- "You waited so patiently while I fed him. Thank you."

You can also tell the baby, in front of your toddler: "Look, your big brother is here. He is so clever." Toddlers often enjoy hearing themselves admired through the baby.

In one of our episodes, Blu leans over his tiny sister and plants a soft kiss on her forehead, all on his own. Moments like that are rarely forced. They tend to arrive after many quiet days of feeling safe and included.

## What not to do

- **Don't overcompensate.** ZERO TO THREE notes that guilty parents sometimes relax every limit or hand out extra treats. Keeping your usual boundaries tells your child that life is still steady.
- **Don't punish regression.** If your toddler wants a bottle or baby talk for a while, respond to the need underneath rather than insisting they act like a "big boy" or "big girl".
- **Don't compare.** "The baby doesn't make this much fuss" plants rivalry.

## Give it time

Adjusting takes time, as UNICEF reminds parents. Some toddlers warm up within days, some take months. If your child seems persistently distressed, or tries to hurt the baby again and again despite calm limits, talk to your paediatrician or health visitor for tailored support.
