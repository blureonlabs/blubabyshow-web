## The short answer

Instead of "stop crying", name the feeling and show you're staying: "You're really sad. I'm right here." If there's a limit, hold it kindly: "It's okay to be angry. I won't let you hit." Toddlers can't switch feelings off on command, but they calm faster when an adult helps them understand what's happening inside.

## Why "stop crying" doesn't work

Most of us say it. It slips out when we're tired, late, or embarrassed, and it often came from our own childhood. There's no shame in it. But it's worth knowing why it rarely helps.

A toddler's ability to manage strong feelings is still being built. ZERO TO THREE notes that most children can't reliably control their impulses until around three and a half to four. So "stop crying" asks a child to do something their brain can't do yet.

The CDC's guidance on emotion coaching is direct about this: telling a child to stop feeling a certain way does not help them understand their emotions. What helps is noticing, connecting, listening and naming the feeling with simple words.

There's also some interesting science behind naming. A 2007 brain-imaging study at UCLA found that when adults put feelings into words, activity in the amygdala (a part of the brain involved in emotional reactions) went down. That study was in adults, not toddlers, so we shouldn't stretch it too far. But it fits with what the CDC recommends for young children: words give a feeling somewhere to go.

## Phrases to use instead

Pick two or three that sound like you. You don't need a script for every moment. You need a few phrases you can reach for when your own heart rate is up.

### When they're sad

- "You're so sad. I'm right here."
- "That was really disappointing."
- "You can cry. I'll stay with you."
- "You miss Mum. Mum will come back after nap."

### When they're angry

- "You're so angry. Your body feels really big right now."
- "You wanted it to be your turn. That's frustrating."
- "It's okay to be angry. I won't let you hit."
- "You can stamp your feet. Let's stamp together."

### When they're scared or overwhelmed

- "That was a loud noise. You're safe. I've got you."
- "It's too busy in here. Let's find a quiet spot."
- "Something feels scary. Tell me, or show me."

### When you're not sure what's wrong

The CDC suggests you don't need to be certain. A gentle guess still shows you're paying attention.

- "It looks like something is bothering you."
- "I wonder if you're tired. Everything feels hard when we're tired."
- "I can see you're upset. I'm going to sit here with you."

## Phrases that hold a limit kindly

Accepting a feeling doesn't mean saying yes to everything. The CDC's model is to accept the emotion first, then set a limit on behaviour. Their example is a good one to borrow: "It's ok to feel angry, but it's not ok to throw our blocks."

A few more in the same shape:

- "You really want more biscuits. Biscuits are finished for today."
- "You don't want to leave the park. It's time to go. You can be sad in the car."
- "You want to hold the baby by yourself. We hold her together, with Mum's hands too."

The NHS points out that giving in to stop a tantrum can teach a child that tantrums work. A calm, warm "no" that you stick to is kinder over time. For more on this, read our guide to [gentle ways to set boundaries with a toddler](/blog/gentle-boundaries-toddler/).

## What to swap, at a glance

| Instead of... | Try... |
|---|---|
| "Stop crying." | "You're sad. I'm here." |
| "There's nothing to cry about." | "That really upset you." |
| "Big boys/girls don't cry." | "Everyone cries sometimes. Even grown-ups." |
| "You're fine." | "That gave you a fright. Let me look." |
| "If you don't stop, we're leaving." | "It's time to go. I'll help you." |
| "Calm down!" | "Let's breathe together. In... and out." |

## The tone matters more than the words

The AAP warns that yelling and shaming don't work and that harsh verbal discipline is linked to more behaviour problems later on. The good news is that the opposite works in your favour. A low, slow voice and a soft face do a lot of the calming before your words even land.

A few things that help:

- **Fewer words.** One short sentence, then pause.
- **Get low.** Kneel or sit so you're not towering over them.
- **Slow your own breath.** Children often match the adult's rhythm.
- **Repeat yourself.** The same calm phrase five times is fine.

## When the tears are about something big

Sometimes crying isn't about a broken biscuit. It's about something huge in a little life: a new baby, a move, a parent away for work. Think of Blu meeting his newborn sister for the first time, teary even as he leans in to kiss her. Happy and wobbly at the same time. In moments like that, you might say, "There's a lot of feelings today. Some happy, some wobbly. All of them are okay."

The CDC notes that children can feel more than one emotion at once, and it helps to name both.

## What if you've already said it?

You will say "stop crying" again. Everyone does. When it happens, you can repair it in a sentence: "I said stop crying. I was feeling rushed. You're allowed to be sad." Repair teaches as much as getting it right first time.

If your child's crying feels constant, or comes with signs of illness, pain, or a change in how they're developing, have a chat with your paediatrician. Otherwise, keep going. Each time you name a feeling instead of shutting it down, you're giving your toddler a word they'll one day use on their own.
