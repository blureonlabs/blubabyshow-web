## The short answer

Most children can name a few colours by around 4 years old, according to the CDC's developmental milestones. Many toddlers start understanding and using some colour words between 2 and 3, but mixing them up is normal for a long time. You can help by talking about colours in everyday life, sorting by colour together, and saying the colour after the object: "the cup is red".

## Seeing colour versus naming it

Babies see colour long before they can talk about it. A Scientific American Mind article by cognitive scientist Melody Dye notes that infants can tell colours apart within a few months of birth. Yet the same article points out that most 2- and 3-year-olds struggle with colour-naming tests.

Why the gap? Seeing a colour is one thing. Learning that the word "green" covers a leaf, a lime and a parrot, but not a teal cup or a yellow banana, is much harder. Colour is not a thing you can pick up. It is a property shared across many different objects, and the lines between colours are fuzzy.

## What the milestones and research say

- **CDC milestones:** the CDC lists "names a few colors of items" among the things most children do by 4 years. That is the age by which most children manage it, not the age when it starts.
- **Research on toddlers:** a 2008 study in the journal PNAS by Anna Franklin and colleagues cites earlier systematic work finding that toddlers acquired reliable knowledge of the words "blue" and "green" at around 37 months, a little over 3 years. In their own study, the toddlers still learning those words averaged 32 months old, and those who had mastered them averaged 46 months.
- **Mistakes last:** Dye's article notes that some children continue to make colour-naming errors well into the early school years.

So if your 2-year-old confidently calls a purple crayon "blue", nothing is wrong. They are in the middle of a slow, normal process. Every child develops at their own pace.

## A rough timeline

These are typical patterns, not deadlines:

- **Under 2 years:** sees and enjoys colours, but colour words are rare. Focus on simply naming colours around them.
- **About 2 to 3 years:** begins to pick out some colours when asked ("Can you find the red one?") and may use one or two colour words, often loosely.
- **About 3 to 4 years:** names more colours correctly, with some mix-ups between close shades.
- **By about 4 years:** names a few colours of everyday items, per the CDC.

## 8 everyday ways to help

1. **Put the colour after the object (2 years and up).** Dye's article describes a study of 34 two-year-olds: children who heard colour words after the noun ("the balloon is red") improved, while those who heard them before ("the red balloon") did not. It was one small study, but it costs nothing to try.

2. **Sort by colour (18 months and up).** Harvard's Center on the Developing Child suggests sorting games by size, shape or colour for 18- to 36-month-olds. Mix red and yellow blocks, socks or plastic cups and sort them into two bowls together. Start with two colours, not six.

3. **Name colours in daily routines (from birth).** "Your shirt is green." "The tomato is red." Getting dressed, cooking and bath time are full of colour talk. No quizzing needed.

4. **Let them pick (2 years and up).** Offer a choice of two: "Do you want the blue cup or the yellow cup?" Choosing gives a reason to listen to the colour word. In one of our episodes, Blu picks his own outfit and walks out in full rainbow from head to toe. Letting toddlers choose their clothes is a lovely, low-stakes colour lesson.

5. **Colour hunts (2 years and up).** Pick one colour for the day: "Let's find everything that is yellow." Look around the home, on a walk or out of the window.

6. **Colour together.** Crayons and paper let children see colours, choose them and hear you name them. "You are using orange. The sun is orange."

7. **Food colours (12 months and up).** Fruit and vegetables are a natural colour chart. Name them as you serve them: "The carrot is orange."

8. **Do not over-correct.** If your child says a green leaf is blue, you can say, "That leaf is green. Green, like your cup." Keep it light. Pressure tends to make toddlers stop trying.

## Things that do not help much

- **Constant testing.** "What colour is this?" all day turns learning into an exam. Mix in more statements than questions.
- **Too many colours at once.** Start with two or three clear colours, like red, blue and yellow, before adding shades like pink and purple.

## When to talk to your paediatrician

Not knowing colours by 2 or 3 is usually not a worry on its own. The CDC encourages parents to talk to their child's doctor if a child is not meeting one or more milestones, has lost skills they once had, or if there are other concerns, and not to wait. That could include not naming a few colours by around 4, alongside other language concerns. Your paediatrician can also check vision if colour mix-ups seem unusually persistent.
