## The short answer

Sensory play is any play where your child explores with their hands, eyes, ears and nose: squishing dough, pouring water, scrunching fabric. You can do it with things already in your kitchen. The two rules that matter most: nothing small enough to fit fully in your child's mouth, and an adult right beside them for every minute of water play.

## What sensory play does

NHS children's services in Cambridgeshire and Peterborough describe messy play as another name for sensory play, and list what it supports: fine motor skills and coordination, thinking and problem-solving, imagination, and social skills like sharing and taking turns. They also point out a quieter benefit. There is no right or wrong way to do it, which is good for a small child's confidence.

For parents, the appeal is simpler. It is cheap, it absorbs toddlers for longer than you might expect, and it needs almost no preparation.

## Before you start

- Lay down an old sheet, newspaper or a plastic tablecloth.
- Dress your child in old clothes, or just a nappy if it is warm.
- Keep a towel and a damp cloth within reach.
- Plan for ten minutes of play and five of cleaning up. Many toddlers like helping to wipe up, so let them.

## 10 sensory play ideas by age

Age ranges are a guide only. Every child develops at their own pace, so watch how your child explores, especially whether they still put everything in their mouth.

### Babies (from about 6 months, with full supervision)

1. **Fabric basket.** Fill a basket with large scraps of different fabrics: cotton, wool, silk, a towel, a clean sock. Let your baby pull them out and scrunch them. Avoid anything with loose threads, buttons or sequins.
2. **Crinkle and rustle.** A clean paper bag or a sheet of baking paper makes a great sound when squeezed. Take it away before it tears into small pieces.
3. **Cool and warm.** Let your baby touch a cool steel bowl, then a warm (not hot) cloth. Name the feeling: "cold", "warm".

### Young toddlers (about 12 to 24 months)

4. **Water pouring station.** Put a shallow tray of water on the floor with cups, a sieve, a funnel and a sponge. The NHS page suggests bath time with sponges, brushes and plastic containers for the same kind of play.
5. **Paint in a bag.** Squeeze a few blobs of paint or coloured yoghurt into a sealed zip-lock bag and tape it to the floor or table. Your toddler can squish the colours without the mess. The NHS page suggests taping the edges to stop leaks.
6. **Cooked pasta play.** Cooked, cooled pasta is slippery and fun to squeeze. Choose small shapes or cut larger ones into small pieces, since your toddler will probably taste it.
7. **Kitchen drum kit.** Upturned steel bowls, a wooden spoon and a plastic bottle half-filled with water make three very different sounds.

### Older toddlers (about 2 to 4 years)

8. **Homemade dough.** Mix flour, salt, water and a little oil into a soft dough. Add rolling pins, cookie cutters and straws. The NHS suggests play dough and salt dough for squeezing, rolling and shaping. Salt dough is not for eating, so keep a close eye.
9. **Flour tray.** A thin layer of flour or atta on a baking tray becomes a surface for drawing with fingers or driving toy cars through. In one of our episodes, Blu takes the flour idea much further than a tray and leaves paw prints all over the kitchen with the Puppy. A tray helps keep it to one spot.
10. **Nature box.** After a walk, fill a box with leaves, large pine cones, smooth big stones and a few flowers. Talk about rough, smooth, crunchy and soft. Check that every item is too large to fit in the mouth.

## Safety notes

### Choking

The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that small round objects, such as balls, marbles, pieces of toys and small round foods, cause the most choking deaths in young children. It advises avoiding toys that can fit entirely into a young child's mouth, and lists coins, button batteries and pen caps as hazards too.

This rules out many popular sensory bin fillers for under-4s:

- dried beans, chickpeas, lentils and dried peas
- uncooked rice in large amounts with a child who still mouths things
- beads, buttons, marbles, pom-poms and water beads
- whole grapes, nuts and popcorn (which the AAP lists as high-risk foods until about age 4)

If you do use small fillers with an older child, sit with them the whole time and pack them away immediately afterwards. Watch older siblings too, as they may hand small items to a younger child.

### Water

HealthyChildren.org warns that young children can drown in as little as an inch or two of water, quickly and silently. For water play:

- Stay close enough to touch your child at all times.
- Never leave a filled bucket, tub or bowl unattended, even for a moment.
- Empty every container as soon as play is over.

### Other checks

- Use food colouring or washable paints labelled safe for children.
- Keep knives and hot pans away if play happens in the kitchen.
- Wash hands before and after, especially with food-based materials.

## When your child does not like mess

Some toddlers love sticky hands; others recoil. Both are normal. You can let them watch first, offer a spoon or a brush instead of fingers, or start with the paint-in-a-bag idea where nothing touches their skin. Never force it. Interest often grows with time.

## Sensory play and a balanced day

The WHO encourages children aged 1 to 4 to avoid long periods of sitting and to get at least 180 minutes of varied physical activity a day.
