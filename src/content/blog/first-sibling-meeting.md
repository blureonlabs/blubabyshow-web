## The short answer

Make the first sibling meeting about your older child as much as the baby. Arrange it early and quietly, greet your toddler with empty arms, introduce the baby as "your" baby, let them touch or hold with lots of support, and keep expectations low. A calm, warm first hello matters more than a perfect photo.

## Why the first meeting matters (and why it doesn't need to be perfect)

The first time your child meets their sibling, they are also meeting a new version of their family. It helps to make that moment feel safe and welcoming. But one meeting does not decide a lifelong relationship. If it goes sideways, with a tired toddler, a crying baby or a sudden fascination with the hospital bed controls, that is fine. Sibling bonds are built over thousands of ordinary days.

Our most-watched episodes on Instagram, at 18.2 million and 12.2 million views, are about Blu becoming a big brother. In one, Blu leans in close to his tiny sister for the first time, eyes shining with tears, while Mum and Dad watch. It is a made-up story, but the feeling it captures is real for many families: that first hello carries a lot of hope.

## Before the meeting

### Plan who brings your toddler, and when

KidsHealth suggests letting your child visit you in hospital as soon as possible after the birth, at a time when there are few other visitors, so it feels like a family moment. Australia's Pregnancy, Birth and Baby service also recommends arranging a hospital visit. HealthyChildren.org, from the American Academy of Pediatrics, suggests having someone bring your older child to meet their new sibling if they are not already there.

Practical tips:

- Choose a time after your toddler's nap and snack, not at the end of a long day.
- Ask the person bringing them to talk about it on the way: "We're going to see Mum, and you'll meet your baby sister."
- Keep the visit short. Twenty calm minutes beats an hour that ends in tears.

### Prepare a small gift

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby suggests a small present for your toddler to open at home or in the car, which can ease a difficult goodbye when they leave the hospital without you. Some families say the gift is "from the baby". Keep it small: a picture book, a soft toy or a toy doctor's kit for "looking after" the baby.

### Have the baby out of your arms

When your toddler walks in, try to have the baby in the cot or with your partner or a nurse, so your arms are free. Your child's first view is you, reaching for them.

## During the meeting

### Greet your toddler first

Give them a big hug. Tell them how much you missed them. Ask about their day. Then, when they are ready, introduce the baby.

- "I missed you so much. Come and sit with me."
- "Would you like to meet your baby brother? He's very small."

### Make it "your baby"

Language matters. "This is your sister" and "She's been waiting to meet you" invite your toddler into a role. UNICEF suggests involving your child in the homecoming, such as letting them tell others about the baby, which builds that sense of belonging.

### Let them explore, at their pace

Some toddlers rush over. Others hang back. KidsHealth advises not forcing it if a child shows no interest, because it can take time. Offer, don't insist:

- "You can touch her foot if you like. Gently, with one finger."
- "Do you want to see her tiny hands?"
- "You can just look. That's fine too."

### Holding the baby, safely

If your toddler wants to hold the baby, sit them on a bed, sofa or your lap, place the baby across their arms and keep your own hand under the baby's head. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby suggests letting a toddler hold the baby with your help, sitting on your lap. Keep it to a minute or two, and stop before it stops being fun.

### Notice what they do

Toddlers love to hear what they did well. UNICEF recommends praising loving behaviour towards the baby:

- "You were so gentle. She liked that."
- "Look, she's holding your finger. She knows you."

## Visitors and photos

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby suggests asking visitors to greet your toddler before they look at the baby. HealthyChildren.org also suggests visitors spend time with the older child, and some may bring a small gift for them too.

ZERO TO THREE suggests not making everything about the baby, including photographing the older child, not only the newborn. If you are taking pictures of the first meeting, take some of your toddler on their own as well: "the big sister on her big day".

## The days after

The first meeting is a beginning. In the following weeks:

- **Expect wobbles.** Your toddler may be sweet at the hospital and stormy at home. That is a normal response to big change.
- **Keep routines steady.** Familiar meals, bath times and bedtime stories reassure.
- **Protect one-on-one time.** Even ten minutes a day of a parent's full attention helps. A hands-on Dad, grandparent or other parent can make this a daily ritual.
- **Never leave them alone together.** Pregnancy, Birth and Baby is clear: never leave a toddler alone with a baby.

If you are still in the weeks before the birth, our guide on [how to prepare your first child for a new baby](/blog/prepare-toddler-for-new-baby/) covers the groundwork. And for the weeks after, see [helping your toddler love the new baby](/blog/help-toddler-love-new-baby/).

If you notice your older child seems very withdrawn or distressed for a long time after the birth, talk to your paediatrician or health visitor. Most children settle with time, patience and lots of reassurance.
