## The short answer

Toddlers feel emotions just as strongly as adults do, but they have fewer words and much less self-control to manage them. That's why feelings seem to take over their whole body. You help most by staying calm, naming the feeling, accepting it while guiding the behaviour, and lending your steadiness until their own grows.

## Why feelings feel so big at this age

Imagine feeling furious, heartbroken or terrified, with no way to explain it and no way to stop your body reacting. That's often what a toddler is living through.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) explains that two-year-olds can swing quickly from cheerful to tearful, and that anger and frustration often erupt suddenly as crying, hitting or screaming. They describe this as a normal part of growing up, and as the only way toddlers have, for now, to deal with the hard parts of life.

Three things are happening at once:

- **Feelings are fully formed.** A toddler's disappointment is real and whole.
- **Words are still arriving.** The CDC's milestones for age two include saying at least two words together, like "More milk." That's a long way from "I'm frustrated because I wanted to do it myself."
- **Self-control is years away.** ZERO TO THREE notes that children usually can't reliably act on a rule until around three and a half to four, even when they know it.

So a big reaction to a small thing isn't drama. It's a small body carrying a big feeling with very few tools.

## What's normal at this age

Every child is different, but these are common and expected in toddlers:

- Quick mood changes, sometimes with no obvious trigger.
- Crying, shouting, flopping to the floor.
- Hitting, kicking or biting when frustrated (which still needs a calm limit).
- Clinginess, especially at separations. The AAP notes that separations usually get easier by around three.
- Big joy too: shrieking laughter, wild excitement, sudden hugs.

It's also normal for toddlers to start noticing other people's feelings. The CDC lists "notices when others are hurt or upset" as a milestone around age two.

## Big feelings aren't only the hard ones

We often talk about big feelings as a problem to fix. But toddlers have big tender feelings too: love, pride, wonder, protectiveness.

Think of Blu leaning in to kiss his sleeping newborn sister, so careful and so gentle. That's a big feeling as well. Naming these moments ("You're being so gentle. You love her.") teaches your child that feelings aren't just things that happen to them. They're part of who they are.

## The gentle way through: five steps

The CDC's emotion-coaching approach gives a simple framework. Here's how it looks with a toddler.

### 1. Notice

Watch for feelings while they're still small: a frown, a whine, a body going stiff. Catching a feeling early is easier than calming a full storm.

### 2. Connect

Make eye contact, come close, offer a gentle touch. The CDC suggests a quick smile or a pat on the back can be enough to show you've seen them.

### 3. Listen

Let your face and tone show you get it. Hold back on fixing or judging for a moment.

### 4. Name

Use simple words: "You're angry." "You're sad." "That was scary." The CDC says you don't need to be certain. A gentle guess shows you're paying attention.

### 5. Guide

Accept the feeling first, then help with the behaviour. The CDC's example: "It's ok to feel angry, but it's not ok to throw our blocks." Then offer a way forward: "Should we build it again, or do a puzzle?"

For lots of everyday ways to build feeling words, see our guide on [how to teach your toddler to name their feelings](/blog/teach-toddler-name-feelings/).

## Give the body somewhere to put the feeling

Toddlers feel emotions physically, so physical outlets help. ZERO TO THREE suggests safe ways to "get the mad out", such as stamping feet, ripping paper or squeezing clay. Other ideas:

- Big, slow breaths: "smell the flower, blow the candle."
- Squeezing a soft toy hard.
- Jumping, running or dancing it out.
- A firm hug, if your child wants one.
- A quiet, cosy corner with cushions and a favourite book.

## Your calm is their training ground

A report for the US Administration for Children and Families, written by researchers at Duke University, describes how self-regulation develops through "co-regulation": caring adults providing support, coaching and modelling until children can manage on their own. It can be taught, they say, much like literacy, with practice over many years.

That means every time you stay steady in a storm, you're not just getting through the moment. You're teaching. Read more in our guide to [co-regulation for toddlers](/blog/co-regulation-toddlers/).

## Small things that make big feelings smaller

- **Sleep and food.** Tired, hungry bodies have shorter fuses.
- **Predictable routines.** Knowing what comes next lowers stress.
- **Warnings before change.** "Two more minutes, then bath."
- **Real choices.** "This cup or that cup?"
- **One-on-one time.** Even ten minutes of your full attention can fill a toddler's tank.

## Look after the grown-up too

Big feelings in small bodies are exhausting for parents. Having your own big feelings in response isn't failure. It's human. When you lose your temper, repair it: "I shouted. I'm sorry. Let's try again." That repair is a lesson in itself.

## When to talk to your paediatrician

This is general information, not medical advice. The AAP suggests checking in with your paediatrician if your toddler seems very passive or withdrawn, constantly sad, or demanding and unsatisfied most of the time. Also reach out if outbursts are very frequent or harmful, or if your child is not meeting milestones or has lost skills. The CDC's advice is simple: you know your child best, so don't wait.
