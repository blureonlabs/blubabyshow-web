## The short answer

Sibling jealousy in toddlers usually shows up as behaviour, not words: clinginess, tantrums, regression or rough hands with the baby. It is a common, understandable reaction to sharing you. The gentle fixes are to name the feeling, hold calm limits, protect one-on-one time and keep the baby safe while your older child adjusts.

## Why a toddler feels jealous

Picture it from a two-year-old's height. The person who used to come the moment you called is now feeding someone else. Visitors coo over the new arrival. The house runs to a new rhythm. A toddler does not have the words for "I am worried I matter less", so the worry comes out through their body and their behaviour.

It also helps to know what the research says. A 2012 review in *Psychological Bulletin* by Brenda Volling looked at decades of studies on firstborns after a sibling's birth. It found little support for the idea that a new baby is a crisis for most children. What it did find were large differences between children: for some the transition is disruptive, for some it marks a developmental step forward, and for many there are few noticeable changes. The clearest pattern was a drop in the firstborn's affection and responsiveness towards their mother.

So if your child is struggling, they are not "failing" at being a big brother or sister. And if they are not, that is normal too.

## Signs of sibling jealousy

Toddler jealousy rarely looks like a child saying "I'm jealous". Look instead for these signs, drawn from ZERO TO THREE, UNICEF and Australia's Pregnancy, Birth and Baby service:

**Regression**
- Baby talk, or wanting to be carried, fed or rocked
- Asking for a bottle or dummy again
- Potty or toileting accidents after being dry
- Waking more at night

**Attention-seeking**
- Clinginess, especially when you are with the baby
- Whining or asking for things during feeds
- New mischief in exactly the moments you are busy

**Big feelings**
- More crying, yelling or tantrums
- Withdrawal, or showing no interest in the baby
- Saying they wish the baby would go away

**Rough behaviour**
- Hitting, poking, squeezing or pulling at the baby

In one of our episodes, Blu waits in a hospital corridor in his Dad's arms, eyes wide, as everyone talks about the baby. That wide-eyed uncertainty is a good reminder that even before jealousy, there is often simply not knowing what all this means for him.

## Gentle fixes that work

### Name the feeling, then the limit

ZERO TO THREE recommends starting with compassion before guiding behaviour. Name what your child feels, then offer a better way to show it.

- "You wish I could play right now. It is hard to wait while I feed the baby."
- "You feel cross with the baby. That is okay. Hitting is not okay. You can squeeze this cushion."
- "You want to be my baby too. Come and sit with me."

Children can feel jealousy without being told off for it. Behaviour still has limits.

### Respond to regression with warmth, not shame

ZERO TO THREE notes that demanding a child act like a "big boy" or "big girl" tends to backfire, and that responding to the underlying need usually helps regression fade sooner. If your toddler asks to be carried like the baby, carry them for a little while. If they want a story while you feed, read one aloud as you feed.

### Turn waiting into a promise

Much jealousy peaks during feeds. Try:

- A "feeding basket" of special books or toys that only comes out when the baby feeds
- Narrating what will happen next: "When she has finished her milk, it is your turn for the tower."
- Keeping the promise, every time you can

### Protect one-on-one time

KidsHealth suggests a daily stretch of one parent's undivided attention, and UNICEF recommends as much one-on-one time as possible, with relatives helping too. This is where a hands-on Dad, grandparent or other parent makes a real difference: a weekly outing that belongs only to the older child can do a lot.

### Praise the good moments

UNICEF suggests praising good behaviour, recognising how much your child has grown and involving them in baby care. "You were so gentle with her toes" says more than "Be nice".

### Keep your usual limits

It is natural to feel guilty and give in more. ZERO TO THREE cautions that relaxing every limit or adding extra treats can send the message that your child cannot cope. Steady routines and familiar boundaries are reassuring.

## Keeping the baby safe

Rough behaviour needs a calm, firm response every time. ZERO TO THREE suggests guiding your child's hands firmly but not angrily and moving them to another activity if it continues. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby is clear: never leave a toddler alone with a baby. A toddler may share food the baby could choke on, or rock the pram until it tips. Stair gates or a closed door to the baby's sleep space give you breathing room.

## When to ask for help

Most jealousy eases as the family finds its new rhythm. Talk to your paediatrician, GP or health visitor if:

- Your child keeps trying to hurt the baby despite calm, consistent limits
- Regression or distress goes on for months or is getting worse
- Your child's sleep, eating or play has changed a lot
- You feel overwhelmed yourself

Asking for support is a sign of good parenting, not a failure.
