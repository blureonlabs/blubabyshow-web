## The short answer

Most babies say a first special name like "mama" or "dada" by around 12 months, try three or more other words by 18 months, and put two words together by 2 years, according to the CDC. You encourage talking mainly by talking: copy your baby's sounds, name what you both see, read and sing every day, and leave pauses for your baby to "answer". Every child develops at their own pace.

## How talking begins

Talking starts long before words. Coos, gurgles, babbles, pointing and waving are all early communication. The NHS describes copying your baby's sounds back to them as teaching "lessons about listening and taking turns". That back-and-forth is the foundation for everything that comes later.

Harvard's Center on the Developing Child calls these exchanges "serve and return". Your baby serves with a sound, a look or a point; you return it with words, a smile or a response. Then you wait for the next serve. It is a simple game, and it is how language grows.

## Typical milestones

From the CDC's "Learn the Signs. Act Early." checklists, most children:

- **By 12 months:** call a parent "mama", "dada" or another special name, wave bye-bye, and understand "no".
- **By 18 months:** try to say three or more words besides "mama" or "dada", and follow one-step directions without gestures, like "Give it to me."
- **By 2 years:** say at least two words together, like "more milk", point to things in a book when asked, and point to at least two body parts.

These are the ages by which most children have reached each step. Some children get there earlier, some later, and many say very little and then suddenly say a lot.

## 10 everyday ways to encourage talking

### 0 to 12 months

1. **Talk face to face.** Hold your baby close and look at them while you talk. Babies watch your mouth and your expressions.
2. **Copy their sounds.** If your baby says "ba", say "ba" back and wait. The NHS recommends repeating your baby's sounds back to them.
3. **Name what you see.** From around 6 months, the NHS suggests pointing at and naming things you can both see: "Dog. A big dog."
4. **Share books.** You do not have to read the words. Point at the pictures and talk about them. The NHS suggests starting picture books in the second half of the first year.
5. **Think about the dummy.** The NHS advises offering a dummy only at sleep times and aiming to stop by 12 months, as it is hard to babble and talk with something in your mouth.

### 12 to 24 months

6. **Say it back correctly.** If your toddler says "nana", reply "Yes, banana!" No need to correct them directly. The NHS recommends simply saying the word the right way.
7. **Offer choices.** "Apple or banana?" Choices give a toddler a reason to use a word, and the NHS suggests them as a way to build vocabulary.
8. **Sing songs with actions.** Action songs and rhymes repeat the same words in a fun way, and toddlers often join in with the actions before the words.

### 2 to 3 years

9. **Add one word.** If your child says "car", you can say "red car" or "fast car". The NHS suggests building sentences a little longer than your child's own.
10. **Narrate everyday life.** Talk through cooking, bathing and getting dressed. Turn off background TV or radio when you can, as the NHS notes that background noise makes it harder for children to listen.

## Families with more than one language

Many families speak two or three languages at home. The NHS advises speaking to your child in whichever language feels most comfortable for you. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child notes that bilingualism is associated with stronger self-regulation skills, and encourages parents who are fluent in more than one language to use them with their children.

## Talking without words

Our show, Blu Baby Show, has no dialogue at all, and that is on purpose. When Blu meets his baby sister for the first time, nobody says a word, but a toddler watching can still point, ask "Baby?" and hear a parent answer. Wordless stories leave space for your voice. That is the serve-and-return idea again: the child notices something, and you put words to it.

## When to talk to your paediatrician

Children learn to talk at very different ages, so a slow start is often nothing to worry about. Still, it is always fine to ask.

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) lists signs worth getting checked, including:

- not understanding what others say, between 7 months and 2 years
- not putting two words together, around 1.5 to 2 years
- saying fewer than 50 words at 2 years

The CDC encourages parents to talk with their child's doctor if a child is not meeting one or more milestones, has lost skills they once had, or if there are any other concerns, and says not to wait. Your doctor may suggest a developmental screening, a hearing test or a referral. In the UK, the NHS says you can also speak to your health visitor or GP, who can refer you to a speech and language therapist.

You know your child best. If something feels off, asking early is never a waste of anyone's time.

## Keep it light

Talking grows from connection, not drills. A few minutes of really listening and answering, many times a day, matters more than flashcards.
