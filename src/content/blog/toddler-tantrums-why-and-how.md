## The short answer

Tantrums happen because toddlers feel big emotions before they have the words or self-control to manage them. They usually start around 18 months and ease by four. The most helpful response is to keep your child safe, stay calm and close, name the feeling, hold any limit kindly, and reconnect once the storm passes.

## Tantrums are normal (really)

If your toddler has thrown themselves on the floor over the wrong colour spoon this week, you are in very good company. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) calls tantrums "a normal part of development" and says they are most common between about one and three years. The NHS says they usually start around 18 months and are far less common by the age of four.

UNICEF India describes tantrums as typical from around one and a half to five years, often about once a day and lasting five to ten minutes. The CDC goes further, describing tantrums as a healthy way for toddlers and preschoolers to release emotional energy.

None of this makes a tantrum in the kitchen at 6pm any easier. But it can take some weight off. Your child isn't broken, and neither is your parenting.

## Why tantrums happen

### The feeling arrives before the words

The NHS puts it simply: toddlers want to express themselves but find it difficult. A two-year-old may know exactly what they want and have no way to say it. That gap is frustrating, and frustration comes out through the body.

### The brakes are still being built

ZERO TO THREE explains that a toddler may be able to repeat a rule ("no hitting") but usually can't reliably act on it until about three and a half to four. Knowing and doing are different skills, and the doing part takes years.

### Bodies run low

Hunger and tiredness are classic triggers, according to both the NHS and the AAP. A tantrum just before lunch or right after a skipped nap is often a fuel problem, not a behaviour problem.

### They want control

ZERO TO THREE also notes that tantrums can come with developmental leaps, as toddlers try to assert some control over their world. Wanting to choose, do it themselves, or say no is a healthy part of growing up, even when it's loud.

## How to respond during a tantrum

### 1. Keep everyone safe

The AAP is clear that if your child is in danger, for example near a road, you step in straight away. Move breakable or sharp things out of reach. If your child is hitting or biting, calmly stop it.

### 2. Stay calm and close

ZERO TO THREE says your calm helps your child regulate. They call this co-regulation (you can read more in our guide to [co-regulation for toddlers](/blog/co-regulation-toddlers/)). In practice, it means lowering your voice, slowing your breath and staying nearby, even if you don't say much.

The NHS also reminds us that shouting back won't end a tantrum. It usually adds fuel.

### 3. Name the feeling

Use short, simple words. The CDC suggests naming emotions even if you're not sure you've got it right.

- "You're so angry. You wanted to do it yourself."
- "You're disappointed the park is closed."
- "Your body is having a big feeling. I'm here."

### 4. Hold the limit, kindly

You can accept the feeling and still say no. The NHS advises not giving in, because children may learn that tantrums get results. Try: "You really want the phone. The phone is Mum's. You can hold your rabbit."

### 5. Let it run its course

When your child is safe, the AAP suggests staying calm and letting the tantrum run its course. UNICEF India suggests giving a child enough space so they can't hurt themselves, and not responding to attention-seeking behaviour during the loudest stage.

### 6. Comfort and reconnect

As the crying slows, offer comfort. UNICEF India recommends soothing your child physically once the tantrum passes. A hug, a drink of water, a quiet "That was hard. You're okay now." Then move on to something new.

## How to prevent some tantrums

You won't prevent them all. But a few small habits, all recommended by the AAP, NHS or ZERO TO THREE, can reduce how often they happen.

- **Protect food and sleep.** Pack a snack and guard nap times where you can.
- **Offer small choices.** "Red cup or blue cup?" gives a toddler some control without a battle.
- **Give warnings before changes.** "Two more slides, then shoes on."
- **Pick your battles.** Decide which rules really matter (safety) and where you can let go (odd socks).
- **Redirect early.** A change of room, a song or a new toy can work if you catch the wobble before it builds.
- **Notice the good moments.** The AAP suggests giving attention and praise for behaviour you want to see more of.

Sometimes the trigger is hiding in plain sight. In one Blu episode, Blu lifts his top and stares down at a grumbling tummy, upset and confused. He isn't being difficult. He's hungry and doesn't yet know what that strange feeling is. A lot of toddler tantrums look just like that from the inside.

## Things that tend to backfire

- Shouting, threats or shaming.
- Smacking or hitting. The AAP advises parents not to spank or hit children.
- Long explanations mid-tantrum.
- Giving in to end the noise.
- Bribes like sweets, which the NHS suggests avoiding.

## Look after yourself too

Tantrums are hard on parents. The AAP recommends taking breaks and handing over to another caregiver if you feel overwhelmed. If you're on your own, make sure your child is safe, step a few feet away and take a few slow breaths. It's okay to need a minute.

## When to talk to your paediatrician

This is general information, not medical advice. Do speak to your paediatrician or health visitor if:

- Tantrums are very frequent, disrupt your child's routine, or cause harm (UNICEF India's guidance).
- Your child regularly hurts themselves or others during tantrums.
- You notice other worries about development, such as lost skills or speech concerns.
- You simply feel something isn't right.

For most families, tantrums are a phase that slowly fades as words and self-control grow. Every calm response you give now is practice your child will one day do on their own.
