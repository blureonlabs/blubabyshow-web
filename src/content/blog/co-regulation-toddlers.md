## The short answer

Co-regulation is lending your calm to your child until they can find their own. When a toddler is overwhelmed, a steady adult who stays close, keeps their voice soft and names what's happening helps the child's body settle. Researchers describe it as the foundation self-regulation is built on, and it's something you can practise in ordinary moments every day.

## What co-regulation actually means

The word sounds technical, but you've almost certainly done it already. Every time you've rocked a crying baby or held a frightened toddler after a fall, you were co-regulating.

A report for the US Administration for Children and Families, written by researchers at Duke University, gives a clear definition. Co-regulation is "an interactional process" in which a caregiver "provides support, coaching, and modeling" that helps a child understand, express and manage their feelings, thoughts and behaviour.

The same report makes three points that are worth holding on to:

- **Self-regulation develops over a long time,** from birth right through to young adulthood.
- **It can be taught, a bit like reading,** with support, practice and coaching from caring adults.
- **It grows inside relationships.** It depends on the co-regulation parents and other caregivers provide.

They use a lovely image: a "bucket" that needs to be full for a child to manage a hard moment. A child can fill part of it with their own skills. The caregiver fills the rest. For toddlers, most of the bucket is still filled by you.

## Why toddlers need so much of it

ZERO TO THREE notes that while toddlers may know a rule, they usually can't reliably act on it until about three and a half to four. Their feelings run fast and their brakes are still being built.

That's why a toddler can go from giggling to sobbing in seconds, and why "calm down" on its own rarely works. They don't have the tools yet. Your calm is the tool.

ZERO TO THREE puts it directly in their guidance on tantrums: adults can't force a child to calm down, but your calm helps your child regulate. They name this co-regulation too.

## How to co-regulate: a simple sequence

### 1. Regulate yourself first

This is the step everyone wants to skip, and the one that matters most. Before you respond:

- Take one slow breath out, longer than the breath in.
- Drop your shoulders.
- Soften your face and lower your voice.

ZERO TO THREE's advice on limits says the same thing: stay calm, use a quiet, steady voice, and notice your facial expression and body language.

### 2. Get close

Come down to your child's level. Sit on the floor if you can. Some children want to be held. Others need you nearby but not touching until the wave passes. Follow their lead.

### 3. Use few, slow words

Name what you see, briefly.

- "You're really upset."
- "That was so frustrating."
- "I'm here. You're safe."

Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes how responsive back-and-forth with a caring adult helps build the brain, including naming what a child feels. Short, warm words are part of that.

### 4. Offer the body something to do

Toddlers often settle through movement and sensation:

- A firm, steady hug if they want one.
- Breathing together: "Smell the flower, blow out the candle."
- Rocking, swaying or a slow walk.
- A drink of water.
- A quiet, familiar song.

### 5. Wait

You don't have to make the feeling stop. You are keeping the boat steady while the storm passes. This is often the longest part.

### 6. Reconnect

When your child softens, offer warmth: a cuddle, a smile, "That was hard. You did it." Then move on to something gentle.

## Co-regulation in calm moments

Co-regulation isn't only for meltdowns. It happens all day, in tiny exchanges:

- Matching your toddler's excitement, then gently bringing it down before bed.
- Naming feelings during play: "Puppy knocked over your tower. Oh no!"
- Predictable routines that help a child know what's coming next.
- Warnings before transitions, which ZERO TO THREE recommends: five minutes, two minutes, one minute.

Think of Blu leaning his cheek against Mum's tummy, quiet and still, listening. Nothing dramatic is happening. But that slow, close, settled moment is co-regulation too. A calm body next to a calm body. These small moments fill the bucket before a hard one comes along.

## When your own bucket is empty

Co-regulation asks a lot of parents, and many of us are running on very little sleep. The Duke report specifically recommends supporting caregivers' own self-regulation so they can meet children's needs. That's not a luxury. It's part of the work.

Some things that help:

- **Step away if you need to.** ZERO TO THREE advises that if you're too angry to respond, make sure your child is safe and take a short break. You might say, "I need a minute to calm my body. I'll be right back."
- **Tag in another adult** if one is around.
- **Repair afterwards** if you lost your temper: "I shouted. I'm sorry. I was feeling cross. You're not in trouble for having big feelings."
- **Lower the bar.** On hard days, safe and fed is enough.

## What co-regulation is not

- **It's not giving in.** You can stay calm and connected and still keep a limit. ("You're upset. The answer is still no. I'm staying with you.")
- **It's not being perfectly calm all the time.** It's coming back to calm, again and again.
- **It's not a technique to stop feelings.** It's company while feelings happen.

## A note on getting support

This article is general information, not medical advice. If your child's feelings regularly seem much bigger or longer than you'd expect, or you're worried about their development or your own wellbeing, please talk to your paediatrician or health visitor. Getting support for yourself is one of the best ways to support your child.
