## The short answer

Prepare your first child gently and concretely: tell them later in the pregnancy, use books and real babies to show what newborns are like, and make any big changes to their routine well before the birth. Above all, keep reminding them, in words and in time together, that their place in your heart is not shrinking.

## Why this feels so big for a small child

Until now, your first child has lived in a world that mostly revolves around them. ZERO TO THREE describes the firstborn as the biggest new variable when a second baby arrives, because everything they know about attention, routine and home is about to shift.

A toddler cannot imagine "a sibling" in the abstract. What they can feel is that Mum is tired, Dad is busy, people keep talking about a baby, and something is changing. Preparation is really about making the unknown known. As ZERO TO THREE puts it, the more familiar the change becomes, the less anxiety there is.

## When to tell them

There is no perfect week, but a few principles help.

- **Later is often easier for toddlers.** Australia's Pregnancy, Birth and Baby service suggests telling a toddler later in pregnancy, when they can already see the bump.
- **Use time they understand.** KidsHealth notes that preschoolers do not grasp months well, so link the arrival to a season or event: "The baby will come when it is cold outside."
- **Let their questions lead.** Give as much detail as they ask for, and no more.

Simple words work best:

- "There is a baby growing in Mum's tummy. You will be the big brother."
- "Babies are very small. They cry, they sleep and they drink milk. They cannot play yet."
- "We will still read our book every night. That stays the same."

## Make the baby real, slowly

Abstract talk does little for a two-year-old. Concrete experiences do much more.

- **Read books about babies and siblings.** HealthyChildren.org (from the American Academy of Pediatrics) recommends reading picture books about newborns and siblings, which also gives toddlers words like "sister", "brother" and "new baby".
- **Look at their own baby photos.** Show them how tiny they once were and talk about who held them.
- **Meet real babies.** ZERO TO THREE suggests pointing out babies when you are out, or visiting friends with newborns, so your child sees what babies can and cannot do.
- **Pretend play.** UNICEF suggests playing with a baby doll and talking about the new baby with warmth and enthusiasm.
- **Talk about "our baby".** It helps your child feel part of the family's story, not a spectator.

In one of our episodes, Blu leans in and kisses his Mum's round tummy, long before he understands who is inside. That small, curious closeness is what you are aiming for: interest, not pressure.

## Make big changes early

If something in your child's routine has to change, do it well before the birth. UNICEF advises making changes such as a new room or a new sleeping arrangement ahead of time. Moving from a cot to a bed, starting nursery or day care, or potty training all count. If those changes land in the same week as a new baby, your child may connect them and feel replaced.

If a change can wait until things settle, it often should.

## Let them help, but never force it

Invite your child to join in: choosing a soft toy for the baby, folding tiny socks, helping set up the cot. ZERO TO THREE warns against making helping compulsory, because it sends the message that your child must feel happy about the baby. Offer, then accept a "no" with a smile.

Useful phrases:

- "Would you like to help me choose the baby's hat? You can say no."
- "You are such a good helper. You found the soft blanket."

## Plan for the birth itself

Young children cope better when they know what will happen to them, not just to the baby.

- **Tell them who will look after them.** "When the baby is coming, Granny will stay with you. She will read your book and give you breakfast."
- **Explain that you will come back.** HealthyChildren.org suggests telling your child you will be in hospital for a few days and will return with the baby.
- **Practise the plan.** A sleepover with the person who will care for them can make the real night feel familiar.
- **Think about sibling classes.** Many hospitals run sibling preparation classes, according to KidsHealth and ZERO TO THREE.

For ideas on the hospital visit and the first hello, see our guide to [making the first sibling meeting special](/blog/first-sibling-meeting/).

## Expect some wobbles, and stay calm

Even with good preparation, most young children show their feelings through behaviour. ZERO TO THREE lists clinginess, night waking, potty accidents, withdrawal and aggression as common responses. HealthyChildren.org advises expecting some regression and, instead of telling a child to act their age, giving them the attention they need.

Try to show empathy while still holding your limits. ZERO TO THREE notes that when a child sees you are compassionate but clear, they are likely to adapt.

What that sounds like:

- "You want to be carried like the baby. Come here, I will carry you to the stairs."
- "You feel cross. You can stamp your feet. You cannot push."

## Protect one-on-one time

The most powerful thing you can give your first child is undivided attention, in small, reliable doses. KidsHealth suggests a daily stretch of one parent's full attention. It does not need to be long. Ten minutes of building blocks while the baby sleeps, a walk with Dad, a bath where no one checks a phone: these tell your child, without words, that they still matter.

Start the habit before the baby arrives, so it is already "our thing" and not a consolation prize afterwards. Ask grandparents and friends to do the same: HealthyChildren.org suggests visitors spend time with the older child, not only the baby.

## A note for both parents

Preparation is not only Mum's job. When Dad or another parent takes over bedtime, nursery drop-off or bath time in the months before the birth, the older child has a strong, familiar person to lean on when Mum is feeding a newborn at 3am.

If your child's behaviour worries you, or seems to go on for a long time, talk to your paediatrician or health visitor. They can help you work out what is typical and what needs more support.
